How We Reach AGI: Why Certification Is Step Zero

25 Sept 2026 · @Erdem · By Rifat Erdem Sahin

Executive Summary

Artificial General Intelligence is not reached in a single breakthrough. It is reached through a staged build-up of verified capability, moving from certified individual competence to certified organizational systems and, eventually, to adaptive machine systems that can absorb and extend that competence.

This white paper sets out a ten-step roadmap (Step 0 through Step 9) for that transition and argues that certification is not a formality bolted onto the front of the process. It is the control mechanism that makes every later step trustworthy. Without a verified baseline at Step 0, none of the automation, agentic delegation, or adaptive systems built in later steps can be safely trusted, audited, or scaled.

Introduction: The AGI Transition Challenge

Most AI transformation efforts fail for the same reason: they start with tools instead of competence. An organization or individual adopts a model, an agent framework, or an automation pipeline before anyone has demonstrated they understand how the underlying system behaves, fails, or needs to be governed. The result is fragile automation that cannot be trusted with autonomy, and a workforce that cannot evaluate whether the AI's output is correct.

The roadmap below solves this by treating AI transformation as a dependency graph rather than a checklist. Each step only works because the step before it produced something verifiable: a certified person, a documented workflow, a public track record, an organized knowledge base, an evaluated agent. Skipping a step does not save time, it just moves the failure further downstream, into production systems that are harder to unwind.

Why Certification Is Step 0

Certification is placed first because it is the only step in the roadmap that produces a third-party-verifiable claim. Everything after it — infrastructure, agents, evaluation, adaptive systems — is built and judged by people, and those people need a reliable signal of who actually understands the technology they are deploying.

  1. Verifiability: A badge from a frontier lab (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta AI) is checkable by anyone: an employer, a client, a collaborator. Self-reported experience with AI tools is not.
  2. Safety alignment: Certification curricula typically require passing safety and alignment modules alongside technical ones. This means a certified builder has been tested on knowing where automation should stop, not just how to build it.
  3. Common baseline: When a team, community, or professional network is built on shared certification (as with the CCAR-P track referenced in this roadmap), members can trust each other's baseline competence without re-litigating it on every project.
  4. Signaling in a crowded market: As AI tooling becomes commoditized, the differentiator shifts from "can use AI" to "can prove they understand AI." Certification is the artifact that carries that proof into a CV, a contract, or a security clearance review.
  5. A gate, not a one-time event: Because the field moves quickly, Step 0 should be read as a recurring practice — re-certifying against updated curricula — rather than a badge earned once and never revisited.

Steps 1-4: Building the Human-AI Foundation

Once competence is certified, it needs a place to operate and a way to be seen.

  • Step 1, New Workforce: shifts team structure toward human-AI collaboration: new roles, and a mindset that treats AI as a collaborator to be managed, not a tool to be ignored or feared.
  • Step 2, Foundation: builds the infrastructure layer — compute, data strategy, and AI infrastructure setup — that everything automated in later steps will run on.
  • Step 3, CV as Code: turns professional credibility into a living, versioned artifact: a public GitHub-hosted resume and track record that updates as work happens, rather than a static PDF.
  • Step 4, Second Brain: organizes the accumulated knowledge (via tools like Notion or Obsidian) so that certified expertise and project history are retrievable rather than trapped in someone's memory.

Steps 5-7: Automation, Agents, and Evaluation Rigor

With a certified, documented foundation in place, the roadmap moves from individual capability to system capability.

  • Step 5, Reverse Training: deconstructs existing systems and workflows to rebuild them through reverse engineering, deepening understanding beyond surface-level use.
  • Step 6, Agents: deploys autonomous agents for specific, multi-step task automation — the first point where AI acts without a human in every loop.
  • Step 7, RAGAS: introduces evaluation discipline: Retrieval-Augmented Generation Assessment, measuring whether the agents built in Step 6 are actually retrieving and generating correct, grounded output rather than plausible-sounding errors.

Steps 8-9: Toward Adaptive Systems and AGI

  • Step 8, AOM (Adaptive Object Model): unifies the evaluated agents and workflows from Steps 5-7 into a single adaptive core: one runtime model that can reconfigure itself as tasks change, rather than a collection of one-off scripts.
  • Step 9, AGI: is the roadmap's terminal goal: systems built on that adaptive core, secured, trained, and capable of high-level problem solving across domains rather than a single narrow task.

Conclusion

Reaching AGI is described here as an engineering and governance problem, not a single research event. Each of the ten steps depends on the verifiability produced by the step before it, and that chain starts at Step 0. Certification matters not because a badge is impressive, but because it is the only checkpoint in the entire roadmap that a third party can independently confirm.
